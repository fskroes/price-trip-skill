# The cost model — what a complete breakdown contains

A total is only trustworthy when every line under it is visible. Work through the
four groups below for every plan. A missing group is a wrong total, not a rounding
error.

---

## Group 1 — Travel

Every ticket that moves the traveller, door to door.

- Home to departure point (bus, train, or the airport train)
- The main leg, **out and back priced separately**
- Airport or station transfer at the destination, **both directions**
- Any leg between airports when the plan uses a mixed-airport pair

The two lines that leak the most money: the transfer to the departure airport, and
the airport train at the far end. Never fold them into "flight".

---

## Group 2 — Bed

- Cost per night × nights
- Always price the private-room alternative on the same line, so the trade is
  visible without a second search
- City tax where it is charged separately at check-in

---

## Group 3 — On the ground

- Food and drinks, at a stated standard. Say which standard.
- One real meal where the trip has an occasion attached
- Local transport at the destination
- One attraction or entry fee if the trip has an obvious one

Budget honestly. A trip priced at survival rations is a number the traveller will
never actually spend.

---

## Group 4 — Fees

- Cabin or hold bag. One night rarely needs a paid bag — say so, it is a saving.
- Seat selection, only if the traveller cares
- Payment surcharges
- Tourist tax where it is not in the room rate

---

## Presenting it

**One table per plan.** Every line above that applies, then a total. No grouped
"transport: €80" summaries — the individual lines are the evidence.

**One ranked comparison table** across plans, with the same four groups as
columns. That table is what the chart in Step 5 visualises.

**Three to five plans, no more.** Ranked by total. Include:

1. The cheapest plan overall.
2. The cheapest plan matching the stated travel preference, even when it loses on
   price. The traveller asked for it, so price it properly rather than dismissing
   it.
3. One plan that moves the dates inside the ±3 day window, with the saving stated
   against the dates as asked.
4. One plan that changes a parameter — usually the number of nights — where that
   change saves real money. Show the saving and the cost of the change.

---

## The date grid, in cost terms

The grid from Step 2b holds fares. Ranking on it is wrong, because every extra
night adds a bed, a tourist tax, and a day of food. Convert before ranking:

```
trip total = fare + fixed ground legs + (nights x per-night cost)
```

State the per-night figure and its parts once, next to the table. Rome, August
2026: €127.50 a night — €60 room, €4 tourist tax, €63.50 food and local transport.

The finding this exposes, almost every time: **the cheapest fare in the window is
not the cheapest trip.** Print the two rows next to each other and let the numbers
say it. In the Rome run, the second-cheapest fare on the grid (€186.81, five
nights) produced the most expensive trip on the page (€889.31), against €513.50
for a fare €4.45 dearer over two nights.

---

## Rules that keep the numbers honest

- **Timestamp every price.** Fares move within hours.
- **Mark every estimate as an estimate.** A hotel band you could not verify is a
  band, not a price. Say which lines are live and which are estimated.
- **Price the logistics failure.** A fare that needs an 05:00 airport arrival with
  no night transport costs an extra night. Add that night to the total, or drop the
  option. Never present it as the cheapest.
- **Report the penalty as a percentage.** "The return leg costs 611% more than the
  outbound" lands harder than two numbers side by side, and it is the finding that
  changes the decision.
- **Correct a total the moment a line item appears late.** Restate it once, plainly,
  and move on.
